Predictive Modeling

Overview

This section presents our unsupervised model for the gender pay question: a K-Means clustering of the IPUMS workforce that groups workers into comparable labor-market segments by earnings and age. We then link those segments to employer demand from the Lightcast job postings through the one dimension the two datasets genuinely share, geography. The clustering complements the wage-gap regression in the companion analysis: the regression measures how large the gender gap is, while the clustering shows where in the wage distribution that gap concentrates.

Methodology Note

The clustering and the state-level join are computed with Spark MLlib in analysis/ml_kmeans_julie.py and analysis/ml_state_join_julie.py, both built on the shared helpers in analysis/spark_utils.py. Running Spark on 1.57 million worker records is too heavy to execute on every site render, so the pipeline writes its results to data/processed/, and this page loads those saved outputs to present the figures. All aggregates are weighted by the IPUMS person weight (PERWT) so the numbers reflect the population rather than the sample. We standardize earnings and age before clustering because K-Means is distance based, and we select the number of clusters by silhouette score.

Step 1: Worker Segments from K-Means

The silhouette score is highest at four clusters, which split the workforce into a large low-wage early-career group, a mid-wage late-career group, a smaller high-wage professional group, and a very small elite-earning group. The table below profiles each segment by its weighted average wage, average age, female share, and the industries its workers most commonly come from.

The clearest pattern is that the female share falls as segment pay rises. Women make up close to half of the low-wage and mid-wage segments, around a third of the high-wage professional segment, and under a quarter of the elite-earning segment. In other words, the higher the pay of a worker segment, the more male dominated it becomes, which is exactly the structural backdrop the wage-gap regression then quantifies.

Step 2: Linking Segments to Employer Demand

We then ask whether this concentration of women away from high-pay segments is softened in states where employers are hiring most actively. Using the state key, we attach each state’s Lightcast posting volume to its worker segments, split states into high-demand and low-demand halves at the median posting count, and compare the female share of the top-pay segment across the two halves.

Code 
sc = pd.read_csv(PROCESSED / "state_cluster_demand.csv")

# Top-pay segment overall, then compare its female share across demand tiers.
top = sc.groupby("cluster")["avg_wage"].mean().idxmax()
state_demand = sc.drop_duplicates("STATE_CODE")[["STATE_CODE", "posting_count"]]
med = state_demand["posting_count"].median()
topdf = sc[sc["cluster"] == top].copy()
topdf["Demand tier"] = topdf["posting_count"].apply(
    lambda x: "High-demand states" if x >= med else "Low-demand states")
tier = (topdf.groupby("Demand tier")["pct_female"].mean() * 100).round(1).reset_index()
tier.columns = ["Demand tier", "% female in top-pay segment"]

The result is essentially flat. The female share of the top-pay segment is almost identical in high-demand and low-demand states, which tells us that the under-representation of women in the highest-paying work does not ease where the labor market is hottest. The gap looks structural rather than something a strong local demand for workers corrects on its own.

Implications for Job Seekers

For a woman targeting the highest-paying segment of the labor market, these results suggest that simply moving to a high-demand state is unlikely to change the odds of reaching that segment, because the imbalance holds steady regardless of demand. The more useful levers are the ones the companion regression points to, namely the occupation and industry a person enters and the unexplained gap that persists even within the same kind of work. The clustering reframes the question from how much women earn on average to which earning tiers they are able to reach, and it shows that the barrier sits at the top of the wage distribution.

Caveats

The Lightcast salary is an advertised figure that signals demand, while the IPUMS wage is what workers actually earned, so the two are reported separately and never averaged together. Employer demand is measured at the state level because the two datasets do not share occupation coding, which means a state’s posting volume repeats across that state’s worker segments by design and supports a state-level comparison rather than a person-level one. The analysis is a single cross-sectional snapshot of roughly 2023 to 2024 rather than a trend.
