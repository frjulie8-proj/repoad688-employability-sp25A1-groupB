Predictive Modeling

Overview

This page presents two complementary models built on the IPUMS workforce data to answer the gender pay question from different angles. The first is a K-Means clustering of workers into comparable labor-market segments by earnings and age, linked to employer demand from the Lightcast job postings through the one dimension the two datasets genuinely share, geography. The second is a linear regression decomposition paired with a Random Forest regressor, which quantifies how much of the gender wage gap is explained by occupation and industry versus how much remains unexplained, and confirms gender’s importance as a wage predictor under a flexible, non-linear model. Together, the two approaches tell a fuller story: the regression measures how large the gender gap is and how much of it survives controlling for job characteristics, while the clustering shows where in the wage distribution that gap concentrates.

Methodology Note

The clustering and the state-level join are computed with Spark MLlib in analysis/ml_kmeans_julie.py and analysis/ml_state_join_julie.py, both built on the shared helpers in analysis/spark_utils.py. Running Spark on 1.57 million worker records is too heavy to execute on every site render, so the pipeline writes its results to data/processed/, and this page loads those saved outputs to present the figures. The features given to K-Means are annual wage income and age, both standardized because K-Means is distance based, and we select the number of clusters by silhouette score. All aggregates are weighted by the IPUMS person weight (PERWT) so the numbers reflect the population rather than the sample.

Implications for Job Seekers

For a woman targeting the highest-paying segment of the labor market, these results suggest that the odds of reaching that segment move very little with how active a state’s hiring market is, and only modestly with its political leaning, where blue-leaning states show a small edge. Across every view the imbalance holds well below parity, so none of these levers comes close to closing it. The more useful levers are the ones the companion regression points to, namely the occupation and industry a person enters and the unexplained gap that persists even within the same kind of work. The clustering reframes the question from how much women earn on average to which earning tiers they are able to reach, and every view here shows the barrier sitting at the top of the wage distribution.

Caveats (Clustering)

The Lightcast salary is an advertised figure that signals demand, while the IPUMS wage is what workers actually earned, so the two are reported separately and never averaged together. Employer demand is measured at the state level because the two datasets do not share occupation coding, which means a state’s posting volume repeats across that state’s worker segments by design and supports a state-level comparison rather than a person-level one. The red and blue grouping reflects the 2024 presidential result and is a coarse proxy for political climate. The analysis is a single cross-sectional snapshot of roughly 2023 to 2024 rather than a trend.

Alongside the unsupervised clustering, this supervised model asks a sharper question: how separable are women and men by their labor-market profile alone? We fit a logistic regression with Spark MLlib that classifies each worker as Female or Male from age, annual wage, detailed occupation and industry codes, state, and race, on an 80/20 train/test split of the IPUMS employed sample. The aim is not to predict gender for its own sake but to measure how strongly gender is encoded in economic outcomes: the more accurately a model recovers gender from wage and job features, the more those features are themselves stratified by gender.

Features used (X) → target (y = SEX, Female vs Male):

Feature Type Description
AGE numeric Worker’s age
INCWAGE numeric Annual wage income
Occupation categorical Detailed IPUMS OCC code
Industry categorical Detailed IPUMS IND code
State categorical STATEFIP
Race categorical RACE

The two numeric features are used directly and the four categorical features are one-hot encoded; using the detailed occupation and industry codes (rather than coarse groups) expands this to several hundred model columns and is what lifts the AUC, since occupation is the most gender-segregated variable. Note that annual wage is intentionally among the predictors: because the project is about the wage gap, a high AUC here partly reflects that wage itself is gender-stratified, alongside occupation and industry sorting.

With the detailed occupation and industry features, the classifier reaches a ROC AUC of 0.83 and about 75% accuracy on held-out workers, well above the 0.50 AUC of random guessing (and up from 0.80 with coarse occupation groups). In plain terms, a worker’s economic profile carries a strong gender signal: knowing someone’s wage, occupation, and industry lets the model identify their sex correctly about three times in four. That separability is the same structural sorting the clustering and the regression describe, now seen from a third angle and measured with classification metrics.

While the K-Means segmentation above shows where in the wage distribution the gender gap concentrates, this section asks a complementary question: how much of the gender wage gap can be explained by occupation and industry choice, versus how much remains unexplained after accounting for the job characteristics available in this dataset. We answer this with two models: a linear regression decomposition (the standard labor-economics approach to wage gap analysis) and a Random Forest regressor to confirm the finding holds under a more flexible, non-linear model.

Methodology Note

Two linear regression models were fit on the full IPUMS employed_only dataset (1.49M workers with positive wages): a baseline model using AGE, RACE, and STATE_NAME, and a full model that adds broad OCCUPATION and INDUSTRY group codes. Comparing the coefficient on SEX between the two models isolates how much of the gap is explained by which jobs men and women hold, versus how much persists within the same occupation and industry. A Random Forest Regressor (100 trees, max depth 10, trained on a 200,000-row sample for computational efficiency) was then used to rank feature importance across all 167 encoded features, confirming whether gender remains a top predictor even in a model that makes no assumptions about a constant linear effect. Full code is available in analysis/ml_wage_gap_antara.ipynb and analysis/ml_rf_antara.py. This analysis uses only the IPUMS employment data, since Lightcast records advertised job postings rather than individual worker outcomes, making it unsuitable for a wage regression on actual earners; the cross-dataset connection between job market demand and wages is explored separately in the K-Means analysis above.

The raw gender gap, controlling only for age, race, and state, is $24,749 annually. Adding occupation and industry to the model narrows this to $22,426 — meaning only 9.4% of the gap is explained by which jobs men and women hold. The remaining 90.6% persists even when comparing men and women in the same broad occupation and industry, suggesting the gap is not simply a story of occupational segregation. Some of this unexplained portion likely reflects factors not captured in this dataset, such as hours worked, seniority, or negotiation differences, but the size of the unexplained share is itself a notable finding.

def clean_feature_label(f):
    if f == 'AGE':
        return 'Age'
    elif f in ['SEX_LABEL_Male', 'SEX_LABEL_ohe_0', 'SEX_LABEL_ohe_1']:
        return 'Gender'
    elif f.startswith('OCC_GROUP_ohe_'):
        return f"Occupation Group {f.replace('OCC_GROUP_ohe_', '')}"
    elif f.startswith('OCC_GROUP_'):
        grp = int(f.replace('OCC_GROUP_', ''))
        return f"Occupation: {occ_lookup.get(grp, f)}"
    elif f.startswith('IND_GROUP_ohe_'):
        return f"Industry Group {f.replace('IND_GROUP_ohe_', '')}"
    elif f.startswith('IND_GROUP_'):
        grp = int(f.replace('IND_GROUP_', ''))
        return f"Industry: {ind_lookup.get(grp, f)}"
    elif f.startswith('STATE_NAME_ohe_'):
        return f"State Feature {f.replace('STATE_NAME_ohe_', '')}"
    elif f.startswith('STATE_NAME_'):
        return f"State: {f.replace('STATE_NAME_', '')}"
    elif f.startswith('RACE_LABEL_ohe_'):
        return f"Race Feature {f.replace('RACE_LABEL_ohe_', '')}"
    elif f.startswith('RACE_LABEL_'):
        return f"Race: {f.replace('RACE_LABEL_', '')}"
    else:
        return f
