Predictive Modeling

Overview

This section presents our unsupervised model for the gender pay question: a K-Means clustering of the IPUMS workforce that groups workers into comparable labor-market segments by earnings and age. We then link those segments to employer demand from the Lightcast job postings through the one dimension the two datasets genuinely share, geography. The clustering complements the wage-gap regression in the companion analysis: the regression measures how large the gender gap is, while the clustering shows where in the wage distribution that gap concentrates.

Methodology Note

The clustering and the state-level join are computed with Spark MLlib in analysis/ml_kmeans_julie.py and analysis/ml_state_join_julie.py, both built on the shared helpers in analysis/spark_utils.py. Running Spark on 1.57 million worker records is too heavy to execute on every site render, so the pipeline writes its results to data/processed/, and this page loads those saved outputs to present the figures. The features given to K-Means are annual wage income and age, both standardized because K-Means is distance based, and we select the number of clusters by silhouette score. All aggregates are weighted by the IPUMS person weight (PERWT) so the numbers reflect the population rather than the sample.

    root = root.parent
sys.path.insert(0, str(root / "analysis"))
from utils import apply_theme, PROCESSED

profile = pd.read_csv(PROCESSED / "kmeans_cluster_profile.csv")

# Label clusters by wage rank so names stay correct even if a re-run
# renumbers the clusters. This same mapping is reused everywhere below.
profile = profile.sort_values("wgt_avg_wage").reset_index(drop=True)
SEGMENTS = ["Low-wage, early career", "Mid-wage, late career",
            "High-wage professional", "Elite earners"]
profile["segment"] = SEGMENTS[: len(profile)]
profile["pct_female_disp"] = (profile["pct_female"] * 100).round(1)
cluster_to_segment = dict(zip(profile["cluster"], profile["segment"]))

How the clusters sit in the data

This scatter shows a random sample of workers placed by age and wage, the two features K-Means actually used, and colored by the segment they were assigned to. The large black diamonds mark each cluster’s centroid, which is the industry-standard term for the center point K-Means computes for a cluster. Reading it bottom to top, the algorithm separates workers into horizontal pay bands: a broad low-wage band, a mid-wage band, a high-wage professional band, and a sparse elite band far above the rest. This is the visual meaning of the clusters, namely neighborhoods of workers who earn and age similarly. A full-data version of this graph that plots all 1.57 million workers is available in the runnable notebook analysis/ml_kmeans_julie.ipynb; the website uses a representative sample so the page stays light.

Worker sample in the age-by-wage feature space, colored by K-Means segment. Black diamonds are cluster centroids. The clusters form distinct horizontal pay bands.

The clearest pattern is that the female share falls as segment pay rises. Women make up close to half of the low-wage and mid-wage segments, around a third of the high-wage professional segment, and under a quarter of the elite-earning segment. The chart below shows the female and male shares side by side within each segment. Because the IPUMS sex variable records only male or female, the male share is the exact complement of the female share, so the two series mirror each other and carry the same information. We show both because the crossover is the point: as pay rises the female bars shrink and the male bars grow, so the two genders trade places between the bottom and the top of the wage distribution. This is the structural backdrop the wage-gap regression then quantifies.

Step 2: Linking Segments to Employer Demand

Step 2 takes the worker segments from Step 1 and asks a geographic question. For every state we attach two kinds of information, the supply side from IPUMS, meaning how its workers are spread across the four segments and how female each segment is, and the demand side from Lightcast, meaning how many job postings that state generated. The single shared key is the state, so the join happens at the state level. The views below move from an interactive map that lets you explore any segment and either sex, to a direct political comparison.

Code 
sc = pd.read_csv(PROCESSED / "state_cluster_demand.csv")
sc["segment"] = sc["cluster"].map(cluster_to_segment)
sc["pct_female_disp"] = (sc["pct_female"] * 100).round(1)
top_segment = profile.iloc[-1]["segment"]          # highest-wage segment
top_cluster = profile.iloc[-1]["cluster"]

The interactive map: female or male share by segment and state

The map below is the supply side made explorable. Use the dropdown to choose any of the four segments together with either the female or the male share, and the choropleth recolors to that selection. The numbers behind it come from a Spark SQL query that computes, for every state and segment, the weighted female and male share in one pass. Whichever female view you pick, the elite-earner segment stays in a narrow band well below parity across the whole country, which is the same nationwide shortage the other views show, now broken out so you can confirm it for any segment yourself.

Code 
ssx = pd.read_csv(PROCESSED / "state_segment_sex.csv")
ssx["segment"] = ssx["cluster"].map(cluster_to_segment)

combos = [(seg, sex) for seg in SEGMENTS for sex in ["Female", "Male"]]
mapfig = go.Figure()
for seg, sex in combos:
    sub = ssx[ssx["segment"] == seg]
    col = "pct_female" if sex == "Female" else "pct_male"
    mapfig.add_trace(go.Choropleth(
        locations=sub["STATE_CODE"], locationmode="USA-states", z=sub[col],
        colorscale="Blues", colorbar_title="%", visible=False,
        name=f"{seg} {sex}",
    ))
mapfig.data[0].visible = True

buttons = []
for i, (seg, sex) in enumerate(combos):
    vis = [False] * len(combos)
    vis[i] = True
    buttons.append(dict(
        label=f"{seg} — {sex}", method="update",
        args=[{"visible": vis},
              {"title": f"{sex} share in the {seg.lower()} segment, by state"}],
    ))

mapfig.update_layout(
    geo_scope="usa",
    updatemenus=[dict(buttons=buttons, x=0.0, xanchor="left",
                      y=1.12, yanchor="top", showactive=True)],
    title=f"{combos[0][1]} share in the {combos[0][0].lower()} segment, by state",
)
apply_theme(mapfig)
mapfig.show()

Interactive choropleth of worker share by state. The dropdown selects the wage segment and the sex. The elite-earner female views stay in a narrow, well-below-parity band nationwide.

Red versus blue states

Because the gender pathway asks about political influence, we also split states by their 2024 presidential result and compare the female share of the top-pay segment in blue states against red states. So that the comparison is self-explanatory, the first map below colors each state by which party carried it in 2024, giving the political geography directly rather than asking the reader to recall it. The second chart then compares the female share of the top-pay segment across those two groups. Here a modest difference does appear: blue states average close to twenty-five percent female in the top-pay segment while red states sit closer to twenty-two percent. The direction fits expectations, with women somewhat better represented at the top in blue-leaning states, but the gap between the two is small and both groups remain far below parity. So politics nudges the result a little, whereas employer demand did not, and neither comes close to closing the structural shortfall the map reveals nationwide.

Implications for Job Seekers

For a woman targeting the highest-paying segment of the labor market, these results suggest that the odds of reaching that segment move very little with how active a state’s hiring market is, and only modestly with its political leaning, where blue-leaning states show a small edge. Across every view the imbalance holds well below parity, so none of these levers comes close to closing it. The more useful levers are the ones the companion regression points to, namely the occupation and industry a person enters and the unexplained gap that persists even within the same kind of work. The clustering reframes the question from how much women earn on average to which earning tiers they are able to reach, and every view here shows the barrier sitting at the top of the wage distribution.

Caveats

The Lightcast salary is an advertised figure that signals demand, while the IPUMS wage is what workers actually earned, so the two are reported separately and never averaged together. Employer demand is measured at the state level because the two datasets do not share occupation coding, which means a state’s posting volume repeats across that state’s worker segments by design and supports a state-level comparison rather than a person-level one. The red and blue grouping reflects the 2024 presidential result and is a coarse proxy for political climate. The analysis is a single cross-sectional snapshot of roughly 2023 to 2024 rather than a trend.
